Late-night radio, midnight to dawn

Small Hours

Hover the words that stand out, or tab to them.

01

Backdrop

02:15, line three

Andrés has pulled over before the tunnel, the engine running for the heat. Tomorrow is his daughter’s birthday, and he won’t be home for it. He asks for a song she can wake up to, and for the weather on the other side.

The tunnel The longest on his route, 11.6 km, and the radio cuts out halfway through
Mila Turns seven tomorrow, and wakes at six to the radio her mother leaves on
The other side −3°, snow before morning
02

Slow Scan

03:10, line two

A red room open to the desert: that’s where Marta will live one day, she tells the host. Below it, a pool under an arch. She has drawn the house so many times she could find her way round it in the dark, past the kitchen and up the blue stairs to nothing.

The living room Facing west, for the evenings
The pool Warm all year, nobody else in it
The stairs For sitting on, mostly
03

Orbit

03:50, line one

Rosa looks after the dancers’ costumes for a theatre that closed in the spring, still pinned for a show that never opened. She can’t sleep, so she has gone in to count the rest: the winter coats from the last tour, folded away in a trunk that still smells of the sea, and right at the back, the suits nobody came back for.

The dancers’ costumes Six of them, cream and white
The winter coats Six, in tweed, brown wool and camel
The suits Six, from sand to black
04

Filmstrip

04:20, line three

A red desert film is on in screen one, Lucas tells the host from the projection booth, though nobody came to see it. Next he’ll run a summer film, all pink walls, pools and sky, the kind nobody makes any more, and last of all, just for himself, an old print so faded it looks drawn by hand.

A red desert film Rooms of red stone, open to the desert
A summer film Pink walls, blue pools, palm trees
An old print Lakes, trees and sleepers in two faded inks
05

Entry

04:40, line one

The small hours are when Valentina writes best. She makes dictionary entries for a living, and tonight she is looking for the right words for the dead of night, the part the host is in now, when even the gulls have gone quiet.

The small hours The hours just after midnight, when everyone else is asleep and the night seems to go on longest.
the dead of night The darkest, quietest part of the night, when nothing seems to move at all.
06

Signal

04:50, line two

By skip, off the night sky, Otto has talked all night to a trawler off Iceland on his own radio. Soon the sky will start to pale, he tells the host, and for half an hour around dawn their voices will ride the grey line. When it’s over he’ll post the skipper a card, as he has after every contact for fifty years: a QSL card.

A signal bouncing off the upper atmosphere and back down, far beyond the horizon. It carries furthest at night.
The band of twilight that circles the Earth. Along it, at dawn and dusk, a weak signal can reach the other side of the world.
The postcard two radio amateurs send each other to prove a contact happened. Otto has kept every one he was sent.
07

On Air

05:00, before the news

The trawler off Iceland left a message overnight, the host says at five, before the news. So did the night shift at the bakery, a lorry driver, two insomniacs. There’s a birthday wish for Mila, who turns seven when she wakes up, and a request for one more song.

Thanks for the company, Otto. Flat sea all night, nets full. Over and out.
Happy birthday, Mila. Papá is on the other side of the tunnel, and home by tea.
After the news, says the host. It’s next.
08

Afterglow

05:15, line one

Camilo minds the monitors of a motorway hotel overnight, with the radio on. On camera two, a woman has been waiting at a red light longer than the light, he tells the host. The laundrette across the road never closes, and down in the hotel’s pool, someone is floating on her back.

Camera two A woman at a red light, in the rain
The laundrette One woman waiting for her washing
The pool Someone floating on her back
09

Instant

05:25, line two

Sofía found an instant camera on the night bus, with three pictures tucked in its case, and nobody has come for it. In one, someone waiting on the yellow seats at the depot; in another, a picnic on a white sheet; in the last, a girl in a striped jumper, not smiling for whoever took it. If its owner is listening, she says, it’s at the depot, under the counter.

Someone waiting on yellow seats under a neon light, at night
A woman lying on a white sheet in the grass, by a basket of flowers
A girl in a striped jumper and a yellow cardigan, against a wall
10

Doors

05:35, line three

A yellow stone on a grey one was the first, Nicolás tells the host, balanced on the sand while the tide was out, for the early walkers to find. By the lifeguards’ tower, where the sand is harder, he stood a red slab on its end; it fell twice and took him most of an hour. The hardest he finished just before he called: five blue stones, stacked against the wind. By noon the sea will have taken them all.

A yellow stone balanced on a grey one
A slab of stone with one red face, standing on its end
Five blue stones stacked into a leaning tower
11

Departures

05:45, line two

Ximena sweeps the station overnight, and from the concourse she reads the host the board as it changes: the first train is already boarding, the mail train is late again, and the night train from the north is just coming in, ten minutes early, for once.

Cádiz
Delayed
Bilbao
12

Scrub

05:50, line two

Pilar runs the photo lab by the station, open all night. Tonight’s rolls are all portraits, six frames of each, as if nobody could choose: a boy with an orange on the salt flats, a woman in yellow who blinked in only one, and one in red, holding something round that catches the light.

Six portraits of a young man in a blue shirt on white salt flats, playing with an orange
Six portraits of a woman in a yellow jumper in the desert, holding a small silver ball; in one, her eyes are closed
Six portraits of a woman in a red blouse in the desert, holding a glass globe
13

Rewind

05:58, line one

Paloma is copying the family’s old tapes before the machine gives out, and she keeps winding them back and forth: her brother’s birthday, the year their mother had to blow the candle out for him, the dandelion she filmed for a whole minute when she was nine, and the dive their father still swears was perfect.

Home video: a mother blows out the candle on her little boy’s birthday cake
Home video: a dandelion in a meadow, its seeds blown away by the wind
Home video: a man in a swimming cap dives into an outdoor pool and swims under the water
14

Slats

06:05, line two

Aurora works nights at the film archive, checking old reels frame by frame before they are copied, and she keeps the best ones in her head to tell the host about. Tonight there was a dancer alone on a lit stage in front of an empty house, then a couple holding each other under a single lamp in a hall nobody had swept for years, and, last of all, a girl in red turning and turning in the rain.

A film still: a dancer alone in a spotlight on a stage, in front of empty seats
A film still: a couple holding each other under a single lamp in an empty hall
A film still: a girl in a red dress turning in the rain under a streetlight
15

Register

06:15, last call

The last caller of the night is Irene, who takes every picture three times, through red, green and blue glass, the way the first colour photographers did. Last week it was her sister in an orange jumper, standing so still for all three that the plates met perfectly, then a pink terrace by a pool, on a day without a breath of wind. Tonight it is a sculpture she can’t stop looking at, and she is laying its plates over each other in the dark, waiting for the colour to come back. Outside the studio window, it already has.

A woman in an orange jumper against a blue wall
Pink steps and white stones by a pale blue pool, palm trees behind
A sculpture of folded coral, pink and green layers
16

Focus Pull

06:30, signing off

That was the last call. From the studio window the host can see the street lamps still burning along the avenue, though the sky no longer needs them. Down at the harbour, the cranes have kept their lights on all night, and the first tram is crossing the bridge with every window lit and nobody aboard yet. To everyone who called, and everyone who only listened: thank you for staying up. Small Hours is back at midnight.

An empty avenue before dawn, two rows of orange street lamps running to a pale sky
Harbour cranes at first light, their lamps mirrored in still water
A lit tram crossing an iron bridge over a river at dawn
